# Exoplanet Transit Method

> The exoplanet transit method detects candidate planets outside the Solar System by measuring periodic dips in a star's brightness as an orbiting planet passes in front of it. Used by NASA's Kepler and TESS missions, transit signals require follow-up analysis to rule out false positives; in November 2026, OpenAI reported amateur searches flagging 200+ Kepler signals and 49 separate candidates.

The exoplanet transit method is a photometric observation technique that identifies candidate planets orbiting distant stars by detecting small, periodic dips in a star's brightness when a planet passes directly in front of it along the observer's line of sight. Used by NASA's Kepler space telescope and Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS), transit photometry has contributed to more than 6,400 confirmed exoplanets recorded through November 24, 2026. Because other astrophysical events can mimic transit dips, flagged signals remain candidates until independent follow-up observations confirm them; on November 24, 2026, OpenAI reported amateur searches using Codex and Astra that flagged over 200 Kepler signals (including 10 known planets) and 49 candidates in a separate search, with one promising candidate awaiting TESS team analysis.

## How the transit method detects candidate planets

When an orbiting exoplanet passes between its host star and an observing telescope, it temporarily blocks a tiny fraction of the star's light, producing a repeating dip in the star's brightness graph (light curve).[2][7] According to NASA and ESA, the depth of the dip indicates the planet's size relative to its host star, while the time interval between repeated dips reveals the planet's orbital period.[2][7]

Space-based surveys such as NASA's Kepler/K2 mission and the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) monitor thousands of stars from above Earth's atmosphere to capture subtle brightness changes and publish public light-curve archives tracked in the NASA Exoplanet Archive and NASA's Discoveries Dashboard.[3][4][5][6]

## How a brief dip in starlight is confirmed as a real planet

A repeating dip in brightness is only a candidate signal, not immediate proof of a planet, because phenomena such as eclipsing binary stars can produce similar light-curve drops.[2][7] Astronomers distinguish candidates from confirmed planets in catalogs like the NASA Exoplanet Archive by vetting light curves and conducting follow-up observations (including ground-based telescopes and complementary techniques such as radial velocity measurements) to rule out false positives and verify planetary mass and orbit.[2][4][7]

## AI-assisted transit searches and the November 2026 OpenAI case

Astronomers have applied machine learning to transit light curves for years; for example, a 2017 neural network trained on 15,000 labeled Kepler signals helped identify an eighth planet orbiting Kepler-90.[1]

On November 24, 2026, OpenAI published a case study describing two amateur astronomers who used Codex and Astra to analyze public telescope datasets.[1] According to OpenAI's report, one search of Kepler data flagged more than 200 signals, of which 10 matched previously confirmed planets, while a separate search flagged 49 candidates, including one promising candidate awaiting further analysis by TESS researchers.[1] Those two totals come from separate searches and represent candidate flags rather than confirmed discoveries.[1]

## Unverified confirmations as of November 2026

As of November 28, 2026, follow-up observations and formal confirmation of any new exoplanets from the November 2026 amateur Codex/Astra candidate searches have not been completed; flagged candidates are not counted as confirmed exoplanet discoveries in the NASA Exoplanet Archive.[1][4]

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the exoplanet transit method?

It is an astronomical technique that finds candidate planets outside the Solar System by measuring small, periodic dips in a star's brightness as an orbiting planet passes in front of it. [2][7]

### How is a brief starlight dip confirmed to be an actual planet?

Because non-planetary phenomena like eclipsing binary stars can mimic transit dips, flagged signals remain candidates until astronomers vet the light curve and perform independent follow-up observations (such as radial velocity or ground-based transit checks) to rule out false positives. [2][4][7]

### Did OpenAI's November 2026 Astra case study confirm new exoplanets?

No. OpenAI reported two separate amateur searches: one flagged over 200 Kepler signals (including 10 already-confirmed planets) and another flagged 49 candidates, with one promising candidate awaiting further analysis by TESS researchers. No new planet confirmation has been announced. [1]

### How many exoplanets have been confirmed overall?

According to NASA Exoplanet Archive data cited through November 24, 2026, astronomers have confirmed more than 6,400 exoplanets since 1992. [1][4]
