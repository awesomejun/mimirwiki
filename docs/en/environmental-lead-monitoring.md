# Environmental lead monitoring

> Environmental lead monitoring measures lead concentrations in air, soil, dust, and water using continuous monitors and laboratory samples to track external contamination and guide public health actions. In the United States, EPA air standards set a 0.15 micrograms per cubic meter 3-month average limit, while CDC uses a 3.5 micrograms per deciliter Blood Lead Reference Value for individual clinical blood testing.

Environmental lead monitoring is the measurement of lead (Pb) in external media such as ambient air, soil, surface dust, and water to identify potential exposure pathways and evaluate compliance with environmental standards. In the United States, federal ambient air standards set by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) limit lead in total suspended particles to 0.15 micrograms per cubic meter as a rolling 3-month average. During acute industrial or ordnance incidents, agencies combine continuous air sensors with pre-event and post-event field sampling across structures, playgrounds, farms, and drinking water intakes. Environmental sampling characterizes external contamination and differs from clinical blood lead testing, which measures lead inside an individual's body.

**Also known as:** Environmental lead sampling, Ambient lead monitoring

## Environmental lead monitoring standards and scope

Environmental lead monitoring tracks lead concentrations across ambient air, soil, surface dust, and water to determine whether lead is present in a community's surroundings.[1][2] Under the Clean Air Act, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) enforces National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) for lead codified in 40 CFR Part 50 and 40 CFR Part 53.[5] Both the primary health-based standard and the secondary welfare-based standard are set at 0.15 micrograms per cubic meter of lead in total suspended particles (TSP) measured as a 3-month average.[5]

The EPA established the current 0.15 micrograms per cubic meter standard in a final rule published on November 12, 2008, and issued a final decision on September 16, 2016, to retain the 2008 standards without revision.[5] Related federal rules cover lead ambient air monitoring requirements updated in December 2010 and the federal reference method for determining lead in total suspended particulate matter finalized in July 2013.[5]

## Environmental lead monitoring methods and sampled media

Environmental lead monitoring combines continuous real-time air instrumentation with physical collection of air cassettes, soil, surface dust, and water samples for laboratory analysis.[2] During emergency responses, teams collect baseline pre-event samples and repeat sampling at the same locations afterward to detect changes attributable to an incident.[1][2] In the November 2026 federal response in Middletown, Iowa, EPA deployed real-time Xact air monitors, air cassettes that sample for lead, Summa canisters for volatile organic compounds, a mobile command center, mobile laboratories, and an air toxics laboratory.[1][2]

Sampling targets areas where residents live, learn, and obtain water or food.[2] In the Iowa response, EPA and contractors sampled homes, over 130 structures tested before and after the event, public parks with playgrounds, schools, daycares, churches, nearby farms in partnership with the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA), drinking water intakes within the modeled plume area, the Mississippi River watershed, and agricultural ponds in Missouri.[2] Samples were sent to EPA's Region 7 laboratory in Kansas City, Kansas, on-site mobile laboratories, and contracted private laboratories for analysis.[2]

## Environmental lead monitoring versus blood lead testing

Environmental lead monitoring measures lead in external surroundings such as air, dust, soil, and water, whereas clinical blood lead testing measures a person's blood lead level (BLL) in micrograms of lead per deciliter of blood.[3][6] According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), two blood collection methods are used: capillary samples obtained via finger-prick or heel-prick for initial screening, and venous samples drawn from a vein to confirm elevated capillary results.[6]

CDC uses a Blood Lead Reference Value (BLRV) of 3.5 micrograms per deciliter to identify patients who require follow-up actions, including education on common exposure sources such as paint in homes built before 1978, contaminated soil, and contaminated drinking water.[6] State public health guidance links environmental monitoring directly to clinical testing decisions.[3] In its November 24, 2026 health alert (IAHAN-0020), the Iowa Department of Health and Human Services (Iowa HHS) stated that post-detonation environmental sampling helps determine where exposure might have occurred and where ongoing risk is highest, and that Iowa HHS uses those findings to update blood lead testing recommendations as needed.[3] Iowa HHS also noted that children younger than 6 years and pregnant or breastfeeding women face greater risk of harm from lead exposure, and prioritized blood lead testing within 48 hours for symptomatic individuals and exposed children under 6, pregnant women, or people with pica or frequent hand-to-mouth behavior.[3]

## Environmental lead monitoring at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in 2026

Environmental lead monitoring drew nationwide attention in November 2026 during the controlled, in-place detonation of degraded crystallized lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa.[1][4] Because degraded crystallized lead azide becomes highly sensitive to friction, vibration, heat, or shock, a multi-agency technical evaluation by the U.S. Army, ATF, EPA, the Defense Threat Reduction Agency (DTRA), and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers approved an in-place controlled detonation by the 71st Ordnance Group on November 25, 2026, at approximately 9:00 a.m. CST.[1][4] Ahead of the detonation, the Army recommended temporary evacuation for Middletown residents and a five-mile cone extending southwest of the plant.[3]

As of EPA's November 26, 2026 update, agency personnel and contractors had collected 1,591 pre-detonation air, water, soil, and dust samples and over 1,000 post-detonation samples beginning around 1:00 p.m. CT on November 25.[2] EPA reported that all real-time Xact air monitors continued to show zero detection of airborne lead throughout the region, and CBS News reported that FEMA and DTRA monitoring similarly indicated no detectable airborne lead following the blast.[2][7] EPA stated on November 26, 2026, that soil and dust sampling was expected to conclude that day and that laboratory results for post-detonation samples were expected to begin arriving as early as November 27, 2026.[2]

## Frequently asked questions

### What can environmental lead monitoring determine?

Environmental lead monitoring measures whether lead is present in external media including ambient air, soil, surface dust, and drinking or agricultural water. Public health agencies use these measurements to map where exposure may have occurred and where ongoing risk exists. [2][3]

### How does environmental lead monitoring differ from a blood lead test?

Environmental lead monitoring tests air, dust, soil, or water outside the body, while a blood lead test measures lead inside a person's bloodstream in micrograms per deciliter using a capillary or venous blood sample. CDC recommends confirming any capillary result at or above 3.5 micrograms per deciliter with a venous blood draw. [3][6]

### What is the U.S. federal air quality standard for lead?

Under EPA National Ambient Air Quality Standards (40 CFR Part 50), both the primary and secondary standards for lead in ambient air are 0.15 micrograms per cubic meter in total suspended particles as a 3-month average. [5]

### What did EPA report after the November 25, 2026 detonation at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant?

On November 26, 2026, EPA reported zero detections of airborne lead on all real-time Xact air monitors, 1,591 pre-detonation samples collected, and over 1,000 post-detonation samples collected. Laboratory analysis results for post-detonation soil, dust, water, and air cassette samples were expected to begin arriving as early as November 27, 2026. [2]
