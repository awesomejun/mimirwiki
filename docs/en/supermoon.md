# Supermoon

> A supermoon occurs when a full Moon aligns with lunar perigee, making it appear up to 14 percent larger and 30 percent brighter than the year's faintest full Moon at apogee. NASA lists the November 2026 supermoon at 2026-11-24 14:54 UTC and the next at 2026-12-24 01:28 UTC.

A supermoon is a popular astronomical term for a full Moon that coincides with the Moon's closest approach to Earth in its elliptical orbit, known as perigee, typically within at least 90 percent of perigee. According to NASA Science, a supermoon occurred on 2026-11-24 at 14:54 UTC as the first in a series of three consecutive supermoons, with the next listed for 2026-12-24 at 01:28 UTC.

## Supermoon definition and orbital mechanics

A supermoon occurs when a full Moon coincides with perigee, the Moon's closest approach to Earth along its slightly elliptical orbit.[1] NASA notes that during each 27-day orbit around Earth, the Moon reaches perigee at about 226,000 miles (363,300 km) and apogee, its farthest point, at about 251,000 miles (405,500 km).[1]

Although 'supermoon' is not an official astronomical term, NASA explains that it is typically used to describe a full Moon coming within at least 90 percent of perigee.[1] Because perigee and the full Moon align only during parts of Earth's yearly orbit around the Sun, supermoons occur three to four times a year and always appear consecutively.[1]

## Supermoon apparent size, brightness, and tidal effect

A supermoon at its closest point can appear up to 14 percent larger and 30 percent brighter than the faintest full Moon of the year, which happens when a full Moon falls near apogee.[1] NASA notes that while a 14 percent size difference is subtle to the unaided eye, a full supermoon is noticeably brighter than other full Moons during the year.[1]

When a supermoon rises near the eastern horizon around sunset, it can look larger still because of the Moon illusion, a common visual effect seen with any full Moon.[2] Physically, the Moon's closer proximity during a supermoon can also produce higher ocean tides than usual.[1]

## Supermoon schedule in November and December 2026

Supermoon listings on NASA Science record the November 2026 full supermoon at 2026-11-24 14:54 UTC and the following supermoon at 2026-12-24 01:28 UTC.[1] Timeanddate.com similarly lists November's full Moon peak at 14:53 UTC (9:53 a.m. EST) on November 24, 2026, noting its traditional name as the Beaver Moon, also known as the Frost Moon or Freezing Moon.[5][6]

NASA's November 2026 skywatching guide explains that the November 24 event kicked off a sequence of three consecutive supermoons spanning November, December, and January.[2]

## Supermoon observation guidance

Supermoon viewing follows the general cycle of the eight lunar phases, which repeat every 29.5 days with a full Moon rising around sunset and setting around sunrise.[4] NASA recommends looking east around sunset to watch the full supermoon rise and provides the interactive Daily Moon Guide for checking daily lunar appearance and viewing details.[2][3]

Actual visibility on any given night depends on local weather and cloud cover at the observer's location.[2]

### Why does a supermoon look bigger and brighter than an ordinary full Moon?

Because a supermoon occurs near perigee (around 226,000 miles or 363,300 km from Earth), NASA reports it can appear up to 14 percent larger and 30 percent brighter than the faintest full Moon at apogee. [1]

### When was the November 2026 supermoon and when is the next one?

### Is 'supermoon' an official astronomical term?

No. NASA notes that 'supermoon' is not an official astronomical term, though it is widely used for a full Moon that comes within at least 90 percent of perigee. [1]

### Why is the November full Moon called the Beaver Moon?

According to timeanddate.com, traditional sources name the November full Moon after beavers actively building winter dams before cold weather, and it is also known as the Frost Moon or Freezing Moon. [6]
