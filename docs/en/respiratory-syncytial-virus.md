# Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV)

> Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a common respiratory virus that can cause severe lower respiratory tract illness in infants, older adults, and people with underlying conditions. In its Week 47 update published on November 27, 2026, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) reported that RSV activity across the EU/EEA remains very low overall but is increasing.

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a common respiratory pathogen that infects the respiratory tract and can cause severe lower respiratory illness in infants, older adults, and individuals with underlying medical conditions. Worldwide, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates RSV causes approximately 3.6 million hospitalizations and about 100,000 deaths annually among children under five years of age. In Europe, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) monitors RSV onset through weekly sentinel primary-care and hospital virological surveillance via the European Respiratory Virus Surveillance Summary (ERVISS); in its Week 47 update published on November 27, 2026, ECDC reported that RSV activity across the EU/EEA remained very low overall but was increasing.

## RSV clinical profile and global disease burden

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a seasonal respiratory virus that commonly causes mild cold-like symptoms in healthy adults but can progress to severe lower respiratory tract disease in young infants, older adults, and people with chronic conditions such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or congestive heart failure.[5][7]

According to WHO, RSV causes an estimated 3.6 million hospitalizations and roughly 100,000 deaths each year among children under five worldwide, with about half of pediatric deaths occurring in infants younger than six months and 97% occurring in low- and middle-income countries.[5] In the United States, WHO cites estimates of up to 160,000 hospitalizations and 10,000 deaths annually among adults over age 65.[5]

## How European sentinel surveillance detects RSV epidemic onset

Across the European Union and European Economic Area (EU/EEA), ECDC tracks RSV alongside influenza and COVID-19 through the European Respiratory Virus Surveillance Summary (ERVISS), combining weekly syndromic reporting and laboratory virological testing from sentinel primary-care clinics and hospitals.[2][3] Similarly, the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) publishes weekly national respiratory surveillance reports and WHO coordinates global RSV tracking under its Global Influenza Programme.[4][6]

## Vaccines and monoclonal antibodies for high-risk groups

WHO notes that while no RSV vaccine was previously available, several RSV vaccines and a long-acting monoclonal antibody have been licensed in recent years and are being introduced to protect infants and older adults from severe RSV disease.[5] Public health agencies such as the NHS provide clinical guidance on symptoms, red-flag breathing signs, and local eligibility for RSV immunization.[7]

## Frequently asked questions

### What is respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)?

RSV is a common respiratory virus that typically causes mild symptoms in healthy adults but can lead to severe lower respiratory tract illness in infants, older adults, and people with underlying cardiopulmonary conditions. [5][7]

### How does European sentinel surveillance detect the start of an RSV epidemic?

ECDC compiles weekly primary-care and hospital virological testing and syndromic indicators from EU/EEA countries through ERVISS to monitor baseline circulation and rising positivity. In Week 47 (November 27, 2026), ECDC reported EU/EEA RSV activity as very low overall but increasing. [1][2][3]

### Who faces the highest risk of severe RSV disease?

According to WHO, infants (especially those under 6 months of age), adults over 65, and individuals with chronic conditions such as asthma, COPD, or congestive heart failure face the highest risk of severe illness and hospitalization. [5]

### Are preventive immunizations available for RSV?

Yes. WHO notes that several RSV vaccines and a long-acting monoclonal antibody have been licensed in recent years to help prevent severe RSV disease in high-risk infants and older adults, subject to national clinical guidance. [5][7]
