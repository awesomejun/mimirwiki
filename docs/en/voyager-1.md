# Voyager 1

> Voyager 1 is a NASA space probe launched in 1977 that explored Jupiter and Saturn and entered interstellar space in August 2012. On 2026-11-18 at 10:16:07 UTC, it became the first human-made object to reach one light-day from Earth (about 16.1 billion miles, or 25.9 billion kilometers), confirmed by NASA on 2026-11-19 via its carrier signal and trajectory calculations.

Voyager 1 is a NASA space probe launched in 1977 and managed by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) that visited Jupiter and Saturn before entering interstellar space in August 2012. ## Voyager 1 mission overview

Voyager 1 is a NASA deep-space probe launched in 1977 alongside its twin, Voyager 2, to take advantage of a rare planetary alignment.[1] Managed by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Southern California, a division of Caltech, the mission launched with 10 dedicated science instruments, and Voyager 1 visited Jupiter and Saturn, returning high-resolution imagery of those planets and their moons.[1][4][6]

After its planetary encounters, Voyager 1 moved above the plane of the solar system and crossed the heliopause into interstellar space in August 2012, becoming one of only two spacecraft (along with Voyager 2) to operate outside the Sun's heliosphere.[4][6][7] Like Voyager 2, Voyager 1 carries a Golden Record containing sounds and images from Earth.[4] NASA Headquarters Heliophysics Division director Joe Westlake noted on 2026-11-19 that the mission contributed both to planetary science and to NASA's heliophysics fleet.[1]

## Voyager 1 one light-day milestone

Voyager 1 reached a distance of one light-day from Earth on 2026-11-18 at 10:16:07 UTC (2:16 a.m. PST / 5:16 a.m. EST), becoming the first spacecraft and human-made object to cross that threshold.[1][3][4] A light-day is the distance light travels in 24 hours at about 186,000 miles (299,000 kilometers) per second in a vacuum, which NASA reports as about 16.1 billion miles (25.9 billion kilometers), or 16.09 billion miles (25.89 billion kilometers) in its mission resource sheet.[1][4] Traveling at about 38,000 mph (61,000 kph), Voyager 1 took more than 49 years of travel to reach one light-day.[1][4]

Because radio signals travel at the speed of light, commands sent from Earth take 24 hours to reach Voyager 1, and signals from the probe take 24 hours to return.[1][2] JPL mission team members received the probe's signal early Thursday, 2026-11-19, confirming the milestone.[1] To mark the event, NASA invited the public to take part in "Shine Bright for Voyager's Light-Day" on 2026-11-17 (flashing the call sign `VGR` in Morse code) and scheduled a moderated discussion at JPL's von Karman Auditorium on 2026-11-19, with listed participants including Voyager project manager Suzanne Dodd and Voyager project scientist Linda Spilker.[3][5] Space.com reported on 2026-11-19 that Voyager 2, traveling on a slower trajectory, will reach one light-day in 2035.[7]

## Voyager 1 November 2026 data interruption

On 2026-11-14, the Voyager 1 mission team became aware that the spacecraft had stopped sending science and engineering data, after a NASA Deep Space Network (DSN) antenna in Canberra, Australia locked onto the spacecraft and received a carrier signal but no data.[1][2] Because the carrier signal remained present, mission engineers were able to confirm the one light-day milestone on 2026-11-19 using the carrier signal and trajectory calculations.[1][2]

NASA's 2026-11-18 engineering update stated that the carrier signal indicates Voyager 1's Attitude and Articulation Control computer, antenna, and broadcast equipment are still functioning, whereas the absence of data suggests an issue with the onboard computer that collects and formats data from spacecraft subsystems and science instruments.[2] Because each round-trip radio exchange takes 48 hours, NASA noted that diagnosing the cause may take weeks or longer.[2] NASA's statements of 2026-11-18 and 2026-11-19 say engineers are working to determine the cause and continue to analyze the issue. As of 2026-11-25, NASA had not announced a cause or the return of science and engineering data.[1][2]

## Voyager 1 power management and 2026 operations

Voyager 1 generates electricity from radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs) that convert heat from decaying plutonium into power, losing about 4 watts each year.[6] In recent years, declining power margins required the mission team to turn off two science instruments; some of the original 10 instruments had already been turned off after the planetary encounters.[6]

In early September 2026, engineers completed a simultaneous swap nicknamed the "Big Bang," turning off three devices on Voyager 1 and substituting three lower-power alternatives to preserve thermal balance and extend science operations; NASA stated that without this swap, another instrument would have needed to be turned off as early as summer 2027.[6] On 2026-10-18 and 2026-10-19, NASA's Deep Space Network was unable to detect Voyager 1's radio signal during two scheduled communications attempts; the signal reappeared on 2026-10-20, about 48 hours after contact was lost and before the team sent troubleshooting commands. NASA noted on 2026-11-18 that the team is investigating whether there is any connection between that October pause and the 2026-11-14 data issue.[2][6]
