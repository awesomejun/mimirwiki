# Hayabusa2

> Hayabusa2 is a Japanese asteroid exploration spacecraft operated by JAXA that returned samples from asteroid Ryugu to Earth in December 2020 and entered an extended mission. After completing a record 400-meter flyby of asteroid Torifune in July 2026, ion engine degradation forced JAXA on November 17, 2026, to replace its 2031 rendezvous target 1998 KY26 with an April 2028 flyby of asteroid 2015 BM510.

Hayabusa2 is a Japanese deep-space asteroid exploration spacecraft developed and operated by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) to investigate the origin and evolution of the Solar System. Launched on December 3, 2014, it explored the C-type asteroid Ryugu from June 2018 to November 2019 and returned surface and subsurface samples to Earth in December 2020 before transitioning into its extended mission (Hayabusa2#) in July 2022. On July 5, 2026, Hayabusa2 completed a flyby of asteroid Torifune at approximately 400 meters from the surface, the closest asteroid flyby on record. Following ion engine degradation and an August 2026 power-supply fault in its remaining operational thruster, JAXA announced on November 17, 2026, that it would cancel the 2031 rendezvous with asteroid 1998 KY26 and retarget the spacecraft for an April 2028 flyby of the primitive D-type asteroid 2015 BM510.

**Also known as:** Hayabusa-2, Hayabusa2#

## Hayabusa2 primary mission and Torifune flyby

Hayabusa2 was launched on December 3, 2014, as the successor to Japan's first Hayabusa spacecraft, targeting the carbon-rich C-type asteroid Ryugu to study the origins of planets, water, and organic materials.[1][3] After surveying Ryugu from June 2018 through November 2019, it delivered its sample return capsule to Earth in December 2020 and entered its extended mission, designated Hayabusa2#, in July 2022 using remaining xenon and chemical propellant.[1][5]

On July 5, 2026, at 18:29 JST, Hayabusa2 executed a high-speed flyby of asteroid Torifune at a relative velocity of 5.3 km/s, passing approximately 744 meters from the asteroid's center and about 400 meters above its surface, the closest asteroid flyby distance recorded to date.[1][6]

## How ion engine degradation reshaped the extended mission

Nearly 12 years after launch, Hayabusa2's four microwave-discharge ion thrusters (ITR-A, B, C, and D) had accumulated 20,153 hours of powered flight, 1.1281 MN-s of total impulse, and 2.044 km/s of delta-V, exceeding the original design life.[1][5] After thrusters A, C, and D degraded following earlier life-extension operations, the spacecraft relied on its last normally functioning thruster, ITR-B, to begin orbital maneuvers on July 9, 2026, toward a December 2027 Earth swingby for asteroid 1998 KY26.[1][2]

In mid-August 2026, ITR-B experienced frequent electrical discharges, and late-August diagnostics confirmed a short circuit in its ion source and a failure in power supply unit 1's ion-source current output.[1][2] Although JAXA engineers verified a cross-mode configuration pairing ITR-D's ion source with ITR-B's neutralizer, its remaining operational lifespan could not be reliably predicted.[1][2] Because the 2031 rendezvous with 1998 KY26 required roughly 650 m/s of delta-V (including about 200 m/s just to reach the December 2027 Earth swingby), JAXA concluded that the original trajectory was no longer feasible.[1][7]

## Hayabusa2 revised April 2028 flyby of asteroid 2015 BM510

On November 17, 2026, JAXA announced a revised flight plan requiring no Earth swingby and fitting within the reliable acceleration budget of Hayabusa2's chemical reaction control system (RCS), which can deliver about 30 m/s of delta-V.[1][6]

| Mission Parameter | Original Plan (1998 KY26) | Revised Plan (2015 BM510) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Encounter type | Rendezvous | High-speed flyby |
| Target arrival | July 2031 (after 2027/2028 Earth swingbys) | April 2028 (no Earth swingby) |
| Required delta-V | ~650 m/s (~200 m/s to 2027 swingby) | ~24 m/s |
| Target characteristics | Small fast-rotating asteroid | Primitive D-type fast rotator |

JAXA selected 2015 BM510 because it requires approximately 24 m/s of delta-V, offers favorable orbital precision and visibility at arrival, and represents a primitive D-type fast-rotating asteroid that no spacecraft has previously explored.[1][5] Planned instruments for the encounter include the telescopic optical navigation camera (ONC-T), thermal infrared imager (TIR), near-infrared spectrometer (NIRS3), and laser altimeter (LIDAR).[1]

## Hayabusa2 unverified outcomes as of November 2026

As of November 27, 2026, Hayabusa2's trajectory correction maneuvers toward 2015 BM510, the April 2028 flyby itself, and any resulting scientific observations or close-range imaging remain unverified future milestones.[1][5] JAXA also notes that the remaining operating lifetime of the cross-mode ion engine configuration cannot be predicted with certainty.[1]

## Frequently asked questions

### What is Hayabusa2?

Hayabusa2 is a JAXA asteroid exploration spacecraft launched in December 2014 that returned samples from asteroid Ryugu in December 2020 and is conducting an extended deep-space mission. [1][3]

### How did ion engine status after sample return affect the new mission target?

After thrusters A, C, and D degraded, an August 2026 short circuit and power-supply fault disabled normal operation of the final thruster ITR-B. Although cross-mode operation (ITR-D ion source with ITR-B neutralizer) works, its lifetime is unpredictable, making the 650 m/s delta-V needed for 1998 KY26 infeasible and leading JAXA to select a ~24 m/s flyby of 2015 BM510 achievable within chemical thruster capacity. [1][2]

### When will Hayabusa2 fly by asteroid 2015 BM510?

Under the revised plan announced by JAXA on November 17, 2026, Hayabusa2 is scheduled to fly by asteroid 2015 BM510 in April 2028 without performing an Earth swingby. [1][5]

### What did Hayabusa2 achieve at asteroid Torifune in July 2026?

On July 5, 2026, Hayabusa2 passed approximately 400 meters from the surface of asteroid Torifune (744 meters from its center) at 5.3 km/s, setting a world record for the closest asteroid flyby. [1][6]
