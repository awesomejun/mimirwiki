# ESA Hera

> ESA Hera is the European Space Agency spacecraft that arrived at the Didymos-Dimorphos binary asteroid system in October 2026 to study the aftermath of NASA's September 2022 DART impact. Initial imagery published on November 25, 2026, revealed a stadium-sized crater across one flattened face of Dimorphos, chaotic tumbling, and hundreds of orbiting boulders.

ESA Hera is a planetary defence spacecraft mission operated by the European Space Agency (ESA) to investigate the Didymos-Dimorphos binary asteroid system following NASA's 2022 Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) kinetic impact. Launched in October 2024, Hera arrived at the binary system in October 2026 to examine the physical aftermath of the collision. On November 25, 2026, ESA and partner research institutions released Hera's first close-range observations showing that DART flattened one face of Dimorphos into a stadium-sized crater. Hera's measurements support scientific calibration of kinetic impact deflection techniques for planetary defence.

## ESA Hera mission purpose and background

ESA Hera is the European Space Agency's follow-up spacecraft mission to NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART), which was the first planetary defence experiment to intentionally alter an asteroid's orbit through a kinetic impact.[1][4] On September 26, 2022, the DART spacecraft, launched on November 24, 2021, on a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California, struck the moonlet Dimorphos at a velocity of 6.1 km/s.[3][4][5] The collision shortened Dimorphos's orbital period around its parent asteroid Didymos by 33 minutes, reducing it from 11.92 hours to 11.37 hours.[1][4]

Although Earth-based telescopes confirmed the orbital change and observed a debris plume extending tens of thousands of kilometres, ground observations could not resolve how the impact altered Dimorphos's surface, shape, interior, or mass.[1][5] ESA Hera launched in October 2024 to travel to the Didymos-Dimorphos system and conduct a close-range post-impact investigation.[5][6] Neither Didymos nor Dimorphos posed a threat to Earth; the binary pair served as a testbed to evaluate the reliability of kinetic impact deflection for future planetary defence.[5]

## ESA Hera target system and observation instruments

ESA Hera operates in the Didymos-Dimorphos binary asteroid system, where the 151-metre-diameter moonlet Dimorphos orbits the 730-metre-diameter main asteroid Didymos at an average distance of approximately 1 kilometre.[1][2] As of November 25, 2026, the binary system was 193.5 million kilometres from Earth in solar orbit.[1]

Following its arrival in October 2026, ESA Hera began imaging the system with onboard instruments including the Asteroid Framing Camera (AFC) and the Thermal Infrared Imager (TIRI).[1][4] On November 7, 2026, Hera's AFC captured close-up imagery of Dimorphos and Didymos from a distance of 29 kilometres at a spatial resolution of 2.7 metres per pixel.[2][5] Additional AFC observation sequences recorded a full orbit of Dimorphos around Didymos from an average distance of 31.6 kilometres at roughly 3 metres per pixel, taking frames every 15 minutes.[4] Scientists from European partner institutions, including the German Aerospace Center (DLR) and the Instituto de Astrofísica de Canarias (IAC), participate in analyzing the mission's data alongside principal investigator Patrick Michel of CNRS and Observatoire de la Côte d'Azur.[1][2][4]

## ESA Hera November 2026 crater and boulder findings

ESA Hera's initial close-range findings, published on November 25, 2026, revealed that NASA's DART impact reshaped one entire side of Dimorphos.[1][2] Comparing pre-impact imagery from DART's DRACO camera with Hera's AFC images from matched viewing angles, ESA and DLR reported that the struck face of Dimorphos was flattened into a sports-stadium-sized impact crater extending across nearly the full width of the moonlet.[1][2] Researchers noted distinctive formations inside the crater, including a cigar-shaped structure near its center.[2][4]

Beyond the crater itself, ESA Hera observed that Dimorphos is now tumbling chaotically and that hundreds of previously unknown boulders encircle the binary asteroid pair.[1][5] In contrast, the larger parent body Didymos showed no major apparent changes from the 2022 impact, with its boulder fields and central equatorial ridge appearing largely unchanged compared to pre-impact observations.[2][4]

## ESA Hera role in planetary defence and ongoing work

ESA Hera's measurements provide direct physical evidence to test and refine computer models of asteroid deflection.[5] Because the 33-minute orbital shift achieved by DART exceeded pre-impact predictions, characterizing the resulting crater geometry, global shape change, boulder ejecta, and rotational state of Dimorphos is necessary to determine how momentum transfers during a kinetic impact.[1][5]

As of November 26, 2026, ESA Hera's investigation at the Didymos-Dimorphos system remains underway.[5] Further close-range observations and final quantitative measurements of the crater dimensions and internal properties of Dimorphos have not yet been published in the initial November 25, 2026 releases.[1][2]

### What is ESA Hera?

### How does ESA Hera investigate the crater left by NASA's DART mission?

ESA Hera uses onboard instruments such as the Asteroid Framing Camera (AFC) and Thermal Infrared Imager (TIRI) to capture close-range images from distances around 29 to 31.6 kilometres and compare them with pre-impact views taken by DART's DRACO camera. [1][2][4]

### What did ESA Hera discover on Dimorphos in November 2026?

In observations released on November 25, 2026, ESA Hera revealed that the DART impact flattened one face of Dimorphos into a stadium-sized crater spanning nearly its entire width, left Dimorphos tumbling chaotically, and placed hundreds of previously unknown boulders in orbit around the binary pair. [1][2][5]

### Was the larger asteroid Didymos changed by the DART impact?

According to DLR and IAC comparisons of pre-impact DART images and Hera AFC imagery released on November 25, 2026, the 730-metre asteroid Didymos shows no major apparent changes, with its boulder fields and central equatorial ridge remaining largely intact. [2][4]
