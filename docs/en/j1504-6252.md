# J1504+6252 (Dún Aonghasa)

> J1504+6252 (nicknamed Dún Aonghasa) is an early-Universe quasar discovered in ESA Euclid Wide Survey data and announced on 2026-11-24. As of that announcement, it is the most distant known quasar at redshift z = 8.13, seen about 630 million years after the Big Bang, with a central black hole estimated by James Webb Space Telescope observations at approximately 360 million solar masses.

J1504+6252, nicknamed Dún Aonghasa, is an early-Universe quasar discovered in data from the European Space Agency's Euclid Wide Survey and announced on 2026-11-24. As of ESA's 2026-11-24 announcement, J1504+6252 is the most distant known quasar and the first identified beyond a redshift of 8, at z = 8.13, corresponding to roughly 630 million years after the Big Bang (about 13.1 billion years ago). Follow-up observations with the James Webb Space Telescope indicate that J1504+6252 is powered by a central supermassive black hole with an estimated mass of approximately 360 million Suns.

## J1504+6252 discovery and naming

J1504+6252 (nicknamed Dún Aonghasa) was identified in roughly 18 months of data from the European Space Agency's Euclid Wide Survey (EWS) among millions of objects and announced on 2026-11-24.[1][4] A team of astronomers within the Euclid Consortium led by Julien Wolf, a postdoc at the Max Planck Institute for Astronomy (MPIA) in Heidelberg, Germany (now at the European Southern Observatory in Garching bei München, Germany), led the discovery.[4][5]

The team nicknamed the object Dún Aonghasa after a prehistoric stone fort on Inis Mor off the western coast of Ireland.[7] After candidate selection in Euclid data, follow-up observations with the W. M. Keck Observatory, the Hubble Space Telescope, the Large Binocular Telescope (LBT), and the James Webb Space Telescope confirmed its nature as a quasar and measured its redshift at `z = 8.13`.[1][4][5] Results were published in *Astronomy & Astrophysics*.[7]

## J1504+6252 distance and redshift record

J1504+6252 is the most distant known quasar as of ESA's 2026-11-24 announcement and the first quasar identified beyond a redshift of 8, with `z = 8.13`.[1][4][5] Redshift measures how much cosmic expansion has stretched the wavelength of light emitted by a source before reaching a telescope.[4][5] ESA and MPIA report that `z = 8.13` corresponds to about 630 million years after the Big Bang, and ESA describes this as seeing the Universe as it was roughly 13.1 billion years ago, 5% of its current age.[1][4]

The discovery broke Euclid's own previous quasar distance record by peering 40 million years earlier into cosmic history.[1][4] In July 2026, ESA and the W. M. Keck Observatory had reported 31 distant quasars from the Euclid Wide Survey (with Keck confirming 21 of the 31); the two most distant were `EUCL J172902.75+641018.1` at redshift 7.77 and `EUCL J125308.55+705432.3` at redshift 7.69.[3][6] As of 2026-11-24, ESA stated that Euclid (launched in 2023) had found 12 new quasars beyond redshift 7, more than doubling the previously known population at that distance.[1]

## J1504+6252 black hole mass and formation constraints

J1504+6252 is a quasar, an intensely bright galactic core powered by gas spiraling into a central supermassive black hole and releasing energy.[1][5] Follow-up observations with the James Webb Space Telescope indicate that J1504+6252 hosts a central black hole with an estimated mass of approximately 360 million Suns.[1]

ESA noted that unlike other distant quasars, Dún Aonghasa is consuming material at a relatively slow rate, and that it must have reached its mass in extremely rapid growth episodes very early in time, which puts pressure on models of early black hole formation and growth.[1] MPIA stated that the discovery supports a scenario in which supermassive black holes form through the direct collapse of large gas clouds and then continue to accumulate matter.[4] ESA and Sky & Telescope describe how such a massive black hole formed and grew so quickly as an open question.[1][7]

## J1504+6252 in Euclid survey context

J1504+6252 appears as a red dot at the center of the Euclid image that ESA published on 2026-11-24; the image covers 30 by 30 arcseconds of sky.[2][7] Distant quasars from the Universe's first billion years are rare and difficult to isolate because their light is stretched into infrared wavelengths where they can resemble nearby cool stars.[6]

The Euclid Wide Survey is planned to cover more than one-third of the total sky once complete, providing wide-field candidates that ground-based and space-based observatories can confirm spectroscopically.[6][7]
